PCA and variance in a direction

Principal component analysis (PCA) looks for the directions along which a dataset varies the most. The data are points \(\vec x_1, \ldots, \vec x_m \in \R^n\), and their mean \(\vec\mu = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \vec x_i\) is the center of the point cloud. For a unit vector \(\vec u\), project each centered data vector \(\vec x_i - \vec\mu\) onto the \(\vec u\)-axis through \(\vec\mu\). The signed projection lengths \(\ip{\vec u}{\vec x_i - \vec\mu}\) are just numbers, so we can ask how spread out they are. Their variance is \(\operatorname{var}(\vec u)\), defined below.

The goal of PCA is to find orthogonal unit directions that maximize \(\operatorname{var}(\vec u)\). The first maximizes it over all unit vectors, the second over unit vectors orthogonal to the first, and so on. These directions turn out to be the eigenvectors \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2, \ldots\) of the sample covariance matrix \(C\), and the variances along them are its eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots\). In the demos below, drag \(\vec u\) and watch the spread change. A density estimate shows how the projections are distributed, and the \(\pm\sigma\) marks show \(\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\vec u)}\).

Definition
The variance of the data in the unit direction \(\vec u\) is the average squared projection length $$\operatorname{var}(\vec u) \;=\; \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^m \ip{\vec u}{\vec x_i - \vec\mu}^2 \;=\; \vec u\vtp C\,\vec u , \qquad C \;=\; \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^m (\vec x_i - \vec\mu)(\vec x_i - \vec\mu)\vtp .$$

Things to try

  1. Turn \(\vec u\) until the dots in the lower panel spread out as much as possible. Then turn on Principal components: the maximizer is \(\vec v_1\), and the largest possible variance is \(\lambda_1\).
  2. Turn \(\vec u\) a quarter-turn away from \(\vec v_1\). Now the spread is as small as possible, \(\operatorname{var}(\vec u) = \lambda_2\). The curve in the sidebar never goes below \(\lambda_2\) or above \(\lambda_1\).
  3. With the ellipse showing, look where the ellipse's shadow on the \(\vec u\)-axis ends. It ends exactly at the \(\pm\sigma\) marks for every \(\vec u\), because the half-width of the ellipse in direction \(\vec u\) is \(\sqrt{\vec u\vtp C\vec u}\).
  4. Flip \(\vec u\) to \(-\vec u\). Every dot mirrors across \(0\), and so does the density, but \(\sigma\) stays the same.
  5. Try the Two clusters data with the Gaussian turned on, and aim \(\vec u\) along the line joining the clusters. The density estimate shows two bumps, but the Gaussian has only one, because all it knows is \(\operatorname{var}(\vec u)\). The variance measures spread whatever shape the data have. The Gaussian is a picture of that one number, not a model of the data.
  6. Try the Nearly round data. When \(\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2\), no direction stands out much, and the curve in the sidebar is nearly flat.

Data in \(\R^3\)

Now \(\vec u\) can be any point on the unit sphere. Drag the arrowhead of \(\vec u\) to move it, or use the sliders. Drag anywhere else in the 3D view to rotate the camera, and click a point to select it. The small sphere in the corner colors each direction by its variance and turns with the view. Since \(\vec u\) and \(-\vec u\) have the same variance, its front half shows every direction.

Things to try

  1. On the sphere in the corner of the 3D view, the brightest spots are \(\pm\vec v_1\) (largest variance, \(\lambda_1\)) and the darkest are \(\pm\vec v_3\) (smallest variance, \(\lambda_3\)). Turn on Principal components to check.
  2. Aim at \(\vec v_2\), then nudge \(\vec u\) around it. Moving toward \(\vec v_1\) raises the variance, and moving toward \(\vec v_3\) lowers it. So \(\vec v_2\) is a saddle point of \(\vec u\vtp C\vec u\) on the sphere, neither a max nor a min. That's why the second principal component is defined as a max over directions orthogonal to \(\vec v_1\).
  3. Switch to the Pancake data. With \(\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2\), every direction in the plane of the pancake gives almost the same spread, and the sphere shows a bright band instead of two bright spots.
  4. Rotate the view until you look straight down the \(\vec u\)-axis. The arrow shrinks to a dot, but the lower panel doesn't change, because the projections depend only on \(\vec u\) and not on where you view from.
